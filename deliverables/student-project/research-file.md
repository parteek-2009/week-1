# Architectural Specification: Probabilistic Bayesian Engine for Bot and Fake Review Detection

## 1. Abstract & System Motivation

### 1.1 Problem Statement
In modern e-commerce ecosystems, bot accounts and paid fake reviewers pose a critical threat to market integrity. Competitors leverage coordinated bot networks to either artificially inflate their own product ratings or execute smear campaigns against rivals. Traditional keyword-matching or purely heuristic approaches fail against sophisticated reviews, while relying entirely on large language models (LLMs) for every incoming review is economically infeasible, non-deterministic, and prone to hallucinations.

### 1.2 Asymmetry of Risk
A fundamental constraint in automated moderation is the asymmetry of risk:
* **False Positive Cost (High Risk):** Banning a genuine customer due to a terse or non-standard review damages brand loyalty, reduces customer lifetime value, and leads to severe administrative friction.
* **False Negative Cost (Moderate Risk):** Allowing a single bot review to pass through slightly degrades rating purity, but can be mitigated over time or caught via secondary auditing.

Therefore, an architecture must heavily bias against wrongful automated bans.

### 1.3 Core Paradigm & Solution
This architecture implements a two-tier **Probabilistic Bayesian Inference Engine** combined with a **Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)** governance framework. By shifting decision-making from subjective text evaluation to pure probability theory, the system:
1. Minimizes compute overhead by using micro-LLMs strictly for lightweight textual classification.
2. Updates belief distributions mathematically using observable empirical evidence.
3. Holds uncertain edge-cases in a "gray zone" for secondary contextual evaluation or human audit, eliminating ungrounded automated bans.

## 2. Theoretical Framework & State Space Model

### 2.1 Exhaustive Mutually Exclusive States
To apply Bayes' Theorem rigorously, the agent models the environment using four hidden states ($S$). These states represent every possible combination of observable perception (how the review appears) and ground-truth reality (what the review actually is).

The state space $S = \{S_1, S_2, S_3, S_4\}$ is defined as:

1. **State $S_1$ (Appears Genuine | Actually Genuine):** Standard authentic review. The user bought the product, used it, and wrote a representative review.
2. **State $S_2$ (Appears Fake | Actually Fake):** Low-effort fraudulent review. Typically short, generic, spammy, or generated by low-quality automated scripts.
3. **State $S_3$ (Appears Genuine | Actually Fake):** High-effort fraudulent review. Synthetically constructed or paid reviews designed specifically to mimic authentic user behavior and bypass superficial automated filters.
4. **State $S_4$ (Appears Fake | Actually Genuine):** Misleading authentic review. Written by real users who leave extremely brief, poorly phrased, or highly specific single-use-case comments that superficially resemble bot activity.

Because these four states cover every permutation of perception vs. reality, they are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{4} P(S_i) = 1.0$$

### 2.2 Aggregate Belief Metrics
The agent evaluates account authenticity by calculating the combined probability of genuine intent:
$$P(\text{Genuine}) = P(S_1) + P(S_4)$$

Conversely, the probability of fraudulent intent is defined as:
$$P(\text{Fake}) = P(S_2) + P(S_3)$$

### 2.3 Base-Rate Prior Assignment
Before observing any specific evidence from an incoming review, the agent initializes its belief distribution using historical system-wide base rates derived from empirical platform data. These base rates serve as the initial prior vector $[P(S_1), P(S_2), P(S_3), P(S_4)]$, ensuring that decisions are grounded in actual historical probabilities rather than arbitrary starting values.
